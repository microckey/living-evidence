Background: the most famous classroom experiment ever run

In 1968, Rosenthal and Jacobson told elementary-school teachers that a test had identified certain pupils as “intellectual bloomers” about to surge ahead. In reality the bloomers had been picked at random. At the end of the year, the researchers reported, those randomly blessed children had gained more IQ than their classmates. The study — Pygmalion in the Classroom — became one of the most cited results in psychology, and its one-line moral, that simply raising a teacher’s expectations makes children measurably smarter, entered textbooks, teacher training, and popular culture.

What entered the textbooks less often: replications began almost immediately, and most of them failed. By 1984, Raudenbush could assemble nineteen experiments that all used the same design — deceive teachers about randomly chosen pupils, then measure IQ. This document carries all nineteen as data, not citations.

What the evidence actually says

Read as one undifferentiated pile, the literature is deflating: pooled across all studies, the average expectancy effect is small and not statistically significant. If the Pygmalion effect were the robust, general phenomenon of popular telling, this is not the forest plot it would leave behind.

But the pile is not undifferentiated, and this is where the story turns. The experiments differ in one crucial, almost embarrassing way: how long the teachers had known their pupils before the researchers lied to them. You cannot easily plant a false expectation about a child the teacher has taught for months. The length of prior teacher–pupil contact explains essentially all of the between-study differences — and once you look only at the studies where the deception had room to work, the effect reappears: in studies where teachers had known their pupils for at most one week, the expectancy induction produced a significant IQ gain.

So the honest one-line summary is neither “Pygmalion is real” nor “Pygmalion failed to replicate.” It is: expectations moved measured IQ only in the narrow window before teachers had formed their own. A claim about the power of belief turns out to be a claim about the timing of belief.

How solid is this?

Two robustness checks are built into this document. First, no single study drives these conclusions — remove any one experiment and the overall picture stands. Second, the evidence base shows no signs of publication bias — though your agent may have opinions about how confidently that can be said. Every claim above carries a deterministic check; none of them has been evaluated yet on your copy of this document. That is deliberate. Don’t take this article’s word for its own claims — have them tested.

    Methods note

    Pooling uses a random-effects model with REML estimation of τ² (DerSimonian–Laird and fixed-effect available through the tools); heterogeneity is reported as Cochran’s Q and I²; the moderator model is a mixed-effects meta-regression on prior contact truncated at three weeks, following Raudenbush (1984); publication-bias diagnostics use Egger’s regression test. The engine is dependency-free JavaScript running in this page, validated against R’s metafor package output for this dataset (all reference values reproduced to published precision; see the test suite in the repository). Statistical caveat: subgroup and moderator analyses here are observational comparisons across randomized experiments and can be confounded by other study features.
